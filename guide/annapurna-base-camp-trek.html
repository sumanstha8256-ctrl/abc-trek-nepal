Annapurna Base Camp Trek: Complete Guide

The Annapurna Base Camp Trek is a Himalayan trekking route in Nepal's Annapurna region that leads into the Annapurna Sanctuary, a glacial basin ringed by peaks over 6,000 m, including Annapurna I (8,091 m). The trail passes through mountain villages, terraced hillsides, rhododendron forest, and river valleys before reaching Annapurna Base Camp at 4,130 m (13,550 ft).

The trek typically takes 7–12 days of walking, depending on the itinerary and starting trailhead. Pokhara is the main gateway to the region. It suits both first-time Himalayan trekkers and experienced hikers, though — because the route passes 4,000 m — altitude, cold, and long walking days shouldn't be underestimated. Spring (March–May) and autumn (September–November) are the most favorable seasons.

What Is the Annapurna Base Camp Trek?

Annapurna Base Camp sits inside the Annapurna Sanctuary — a glacial basin entered through the narrow Modi Khola gorge, enclosed by towering peaks including Annapurna I, Annapurna South, Hiunchuli, and Machhapuchhre. The trek is defined by that destination: a dramatic mountain amphitheater reached through several distinct landscape zones.

Where Is Annapurna Base Camp?

Annapurna Base Camp is situated in north-central Nepal within the Gandaki Province, approximately 40 kilometers north of Pokhara in a straight line. It occupies the floor of the Annapurna Sanctuary at 4,130 meters, surrounded by the Annapurna mountain range inside the protected borders of the Annapurna Conservation Area.

What Makes the ABC Trek Special?

The trek compresses subtropical forest, rhododendron woodland, Gurung and Magar mountain villages, river valleys, natural hot springs, and high alpine terrain into one 7–12 day route — a wider range of scenery than most treks of comparable length, without requiring technical climbing skill.

Unlike Everest Base Camp, there is no flight into a weather-dependent mountain airstrip like Lukla; the trailhead is a road drive from Pokhara, which makes the trek logistically simpler to start and end on schedule without flight cancellation risks.

Who Is This Trek Best For?

Trekkers with reasonable fitness and multi-day walking stamina, whether or not they have prior Himalayan experience. It doesn't demand technical skill, but does demand sustained daily walking at altitude — see the Difficulty section below for what "moderate" actually means on this trail.

Route, Elevation, and Landscape

Where Does the ABC Trek Start and End?

Most modern itineraries take a private 4WD jeep directly to roadheads such as Siwai or Matque (1,400–1,500 m) or Kimche/Ghandruk (1,600–1,940 m), cutting out the dusty gravel-road walk from the older Nayapul trailhead (1,070 m). Treks generally exit via Jhinu Danda and the suspension bridge to the Matque/Siwai jeep stand for the return drive to Pokhara.

The Main Trail Progression

Roadhead (Siwai/Matque or Kimche/Ghandruk) → Chhomrong → Sinuwa → Bamboo → Dovan → Deurali → Machhapuchhre Base Camp → Annapurna Base Camp (4,130 m), then back down via Bamboo and Jhinu Danda to the jeep roadhead and Pokhara.

This is a there-and-back walk through the Modi Khola valley, not a loop — the descent largely retraces the ascent, though it typically moves faster since altitude is no longer a limiting factor.

Main Villages and Stops Along the Route

  • Ghandruk (1,940 m): A large, historic Gurung village with slate-roofed houses, mountain museums, and entry trailheads.
  • Chhomrong (2,170 m): The last major permanent village before the gorge narrows, famous for its stone staircases and bakery lodges.
  • Sinuwa, Bamboo, Dovan & Deurali (2,340–3,230 m): Smaller lodge clusters through the dense bamboo, rhododendron forest, and alpine gateway sections.
  • Machhapuchhre Base Camp (MBC, 3,700 m): The threshold to the Sanctuary, offering direct perspectives of Fishtail.
  • Annapurna Base Camp (ABC, 4,130 m): The glacial bowl terminus encircled 360 degrees by 7,000m and 8,000m giants.
  • Jhinu Danda (1,780 m): Located on the return descent, celebrated for its natural riverside geothermal hot springs.

Landscape by Elevation

  • Subtropical forest and terraced farmland (lower trail, ~1,000–2,000 m): Warm, humid, village-dense with agricultural terraces.
  • Rhododendron and oak forest (mid-elevation, ~2,000–3,000 m): Cooler, denser tree canopy alive with birdlife and moss.
  • Alpine zone (~3,000–3,700 m): More exposed, rockier, far fewer trees, and noticeably windier.
  • High-altitude sanctuary (3,700–4,130 m): Glacial moraines, alpine tundra, crisp cold air, mountain-enclosed on all sides.

Elevation Gain Analysis

The trek entails roughly 3,000 m net elevation gain, from a 1,400–1,940 m jeep roadhead to 4,130 m at base camp. Most days gain 300–600 m; the push from Deurali (3,230 m) to Annapurna Base Camp (4,130 m) gains 900 m — the biggest single-day altitude jump on the trek, and the point where acclimatization matters most.

Terrain Reality: Stone Steps vs. Altitude

The incline through the glacial basin between Machhapuchhre Base Camp and ABC is a gradual, gentle climb. The physically hardest terrain on the whole route is actually the stone-staircase descent and re-ascent between Chhomrong and Sinuwa — thousands of steep, uneven steps. This uneven distribution is intentional in a well-built itinerary: gradual early days let your body adjust before the altitude push near the end.

Total Distance: A reasonable planning range is 55–80 km round-trip, depending on trailhead and route. Plan around this range and confirm the specific distance for your chosen itinerary with your agency.

Can it be shorter or longer? Yes on both ends. A minimal 7-day version (driving further in, skipping Poon Hill) is achievable for time-constrained trekkers with good fitness. Extending toward 12–14 days by adding Poon Hill, extra acclimatization days, or a slower pace suits first-timers or anyone prioritizing comfort over speed.

Annapurna Base Camp Trek Difficulty

Official Rating: Moderate. No technical climbing skill is required, but the trek combines steep stone staircases, several consecutive 5–8 hour walking days, and a high-altitude finish above 4,000 m. This is a considered rating reflecting terrain, altitude factors, and how the route is classified by licensed agencies.

What Makes the Trek Demanding?

  • Elevation: The ~3,000 m net gain, concentrated into a relatively short trip.
  • Steep stone steps: Particularly on the ascent sections between lower villages, where staircases can run for hundreds of vertical meters with little flat ground to recover on.
  • Long walking days: Several days run 5–8 hours, and the descent day frequently runs longer still.
  • Altitude above 3,000 m: Thinner air on top of an already tiring uphill push.
  • Descents: Repeated downhill impact stresses knees and quads more than most trekkers expect; treating descent days as "the easy half" is a common and avoidable mistake.

Fitness Benchmark

A reasonable target before departure is being able to walk 8–10 km with 400–500 m of elevation gain without needing an extended recovery, and to climb multiple flights of stairs carrying a light daypack without stopping. If that's already comfortable through regular walking, hiking, or sports, you're likely ready.

How to Train for the ABC Trek (4–6 Weeks Beforehand)

  • Cardio conditioning: 3–4 weekly cardio sessions (walking, hiking, cycling, or light running) to build aerobic endurance.
  • Stair & incline climbing: Dedicated stair-master or outdoor hill climbing to build the specific quadricep and calf strength required for thousands of stone steps.
  • Loaded daypack walks: 1–2 weekly hikes carrying a 5–7 kg pack to condition your shoulders and core.
  • Downhill eccentric training: Step-downs, lunges, and downhill hiking practice — descent-related fatigue and knee strain catch more trekkers off guard than climbing does.

Is the ABC Trek Suitable for Beginners?

The trek is achievable for first-timers with reasonable fitness — regular walking or hiking ability, comfort on stairs and inclines, and a realistic training period beforehand. It is not a trek to attempt with zero physical preparation, but it also does not require prior Himalayan or mountaineering experience.

Best Time for Annapurna Base Camp Trek

Season Weather, Mountain Visibility & Trail Conditions
Spring (Mar–May) Mild temperatures, rhododendrons in bloom, generally stable weather; March can still carry cooler conditions, while April–May offer warm days and vivid forest colors.
Monsoon (Jun–Aug) Heavy rain, muddy trails, leeches in the forest, limited mountain visibility. Technically possible but not recommended for most international trekkers.
Autumn (Sep–Nov) The premier trekking season. September is transitional; October and November bring the clearest, most stable high-pressure skies of the year.
Winter (Dec–Feb) Cold, crisp, and quiet with fewer trekkers. Snow can occasionally close the final MBC–ABC canyon, and high lodges operate with skeleton staff.

Spring in Detail (March to May)

Daytime temperatures at lower elevations reach 15–20°C, with rhododendron forests blooming most vividly through April. Nights near base camp still drop close to freezing regardless of season.

Autumn in Detail (September to November)

October and November offer the most consistent clear-sky days of the year, making them the highest-demand months for teahouse bookings. September sits at the monsoon-to-autumn transition — genuinely different in the first half of the month versus the second, with rain risk early on and some of the year's clearest views once the monsoon fully withdraws.

Monsoon Benchmark: October 2

Nepal's monsoon officially runs June through September, with the Department of Hydrology and Meteorology's normal withdrawal benchmark at October 2 — later than many trekkers assume, which is why early autumn departures can still carry residual rain showers.

Can You Trek ABC in Winter?

Yes, with caveats: lower-elevation days are often clear and dry, but snow can occasionally close the Machhapuchhre Base Camp–ABC section, and some teahouses above Chhomrong reduce services or close in the deepest winter months. Always confirm trail and lodge status with your registered agency if trekking December through February.

Annapurna Base Camp Trek Weather

Weather varies sharply by elevation on this trek, more than most first-time trekkers expect. Lower down, near Pokhara and Ghandruk, daytime temperatures can reach 20–28°C with real humidity; by the time you reach Annapurna Base Camp (4,130 m), daytime highs drop to roughly 5–10°C with nights consistently at or below freezing (-2°C to -10°C), regardless of season. That range is why layered clothing matters more here than any single heavy jacket.

Mountain Visibility Pattern: Visibility is generally best in early morning before afternoon cloud build-up — a pattern true across most seasons. Viewpoint mornings (sunrise over Annapurna I and Fishtail) are always scheduled early. Weather changes quickly at altitude: a crystal-clear morning doesn't guarantee a clear afternoon, so adjust timing and pace accordingly.

Altitude and Acclimatization

Annapurna Base Camp's 4,130 m elevation puts trekkers above the altitude where mild Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS) becomes a real risk. General medical guidance identifies 2,500 meters as the threshold where altitude physiology begins to change — a benchmark you pass well before reaching base camp.

Common AMS Symptoms

A persistent headache combined with at least one of: nausea, unusual fatigue, dizziness, or disrupted sleep — usually appearing within 6–12 hours of reaching a new altitude.

Critical Warning Signs Requiring Immediate Descent

Confusion, loss of coordination (ataxia / stumbling), severe breathlessness at rest, or a rattling cough/choking sensation at night (signs of HACE/HAPE). In these scenarios, immediate descent of 500–1,000 meters is non-negotiable.

Essential Rules on Managing Altitude

  • Fitness does not prevent AMS: A marathon runner can develop altitude sickness just as easily as a casual hiker. Risk factors are ascent speed, altitude gained, and prior history, not fitness level.
  • Never ascend with symptoms: Mild symptoms call for rest at the same altitude, hydration (3–4 liters daily), and holding off on climbing higher.
  • Descent is the cure: Worsening symptoms or any serious warning sign require immediate descent. Descending even 300–500 meters provides rapid, life-saving physiological relief.

Annapurna Base Camp Trek Cost

A fully guided Annapurna Base Camp trek package typically runs USD $600 to $1,300 per person, varying by group size, season, and service tier. On-trail out-of-pocket expenses (meals and teahouse lodging, excluding agency, guide, and porter fees) typically run USD $35–50 per day.

Because fully independent (unguided) trekking is prohibited for foreign nationals in this region (see Permits below), every trekker arranges a licensed guide through a registered agency.

Annapurna Base Camp Trek Permits

Two primary requirements govern trail access: the official ACAP permit, and a licensed guide booked through a registered agency. TIMS status is more nuanced than many sources present it.

Requirement Cost Current Status & Enforcement
ACAP (Annapurna Conservation Area Permit) NPR 3,000 (~USD $23) foreign nationals; NPR 1,000 SAARC; free under 10 Required and enforced at all checkpoints (Birethanti, Chhomrong, Ghorepani).
TIMS Card NPR 2,000 (foreigners); NPR 1,000 SAARC Reclassified, not abolished. The old green solo-trekker TIMS was retired when unguided trekking ended in April 2023. An Agency/Group TIMS is still officially required by TAAN/NTB regulations and is processed electronically by your registered agency.
Licensed Guide Included in package Mandatory since April 2023 by Nepal Tourism Board regulations; must be arranged through a registered agency.

Where to obtain ACAP: ACAP can be obtained online through the NTNC e-permit portal (epermit.ntnc.org.np) or in person at NTNC / Nepal Tourism Board offices in Kathmandu or Pokhara. You will need your passport, passport photos, and trekking itinerary.

Checkpoint Penalty Warning

Always secure your ACAP permit before reaching the trailhead. Purchasing it at trail checkpoints such as Birethanti triggers a 100% late-purchase penalty that doubles the fee from NPR 3,000 to roughly NPR 6,000. When you book a guided trek with ABC Trek in Nepal, all permits and fees are fully managed for you in advance.

Accommodation on the ABC Trek

Teahouses run the entire route, offering mostly twin-share rooms with wooden beds, foam mattresses, and shared common dining halls. Comfort naturally decreases as elevation rises:

  • Lower elevations (Ghandruk, Chhomrong): Attached bathrooms and solar/gas hot showers are common; Wi-Fi is generally dependable.
  • Mid-route (Sinuwa, Bamboo, Dovan, Deurali): Simpler rooms, shared western or squat toilets, hot showers less reliable.
  • Near Annapurna Base Camp (MBC & ABC): Basic — twin beds, pillow, blanket, shared facilities, and limited or no hot running water due to freezing conditions.

Advance booking matters most in peak autumn (October–November) when lodge occupancy reaches capacity. On a guided trek, your mountain guide arranges rooms ahead of each day's arrival.

Food and Drinking Water

Dal Bhat (steamed rice, spiced lentil soup, seasonal vegetable curry, and pickle — almost always with complimentary refills) is the fuel of the Himalayas. It is freshly cooked, nutritious, and hearty.

Breakfast menus offer porridge, eggs, muesli, and Tibetan flatbread. Other dishes (fried noodles, pasta, momos, garlic soup) are widely available.

Drinking Water: Safe drinking water is available at every lodge. Rather than buying single-use plastic bottles (which cannot be recycled at altitude and harm fragile alpine ecosystems), carry a reusable bottle or hydration bladder paired with water purification tablets or a hollow-fiber filter (e.g., Sawyer Squeeze).

Guide and Porter for ABC Trek

A licensed guide is legally required for every foreign trekker in the Annapurna Conservation Area. Beyond legal compliance, an experienced guide provides critical route navigation, trail-condition intelligence, cultural liaison with teahouse hosts, and daily wilderness first aid and altitude assessment.

Porter Services: A porter is optional but highly recommended. In accordance with strict ethical porter welfare guidelines (IPPG standards), porters carry a maximum weight limit of 15–20 kg, usually shared between two trekkers (10 kg per trekker). Walking with only a light daypack significantly reduces fatigue and knee stress on steep staircase sections.

Annapurna Base Camp Trek Packing List

The temperature range across this trek (25°C at the trailhead to below freezing at base camp) demands an intelligent, modular layering strategy:

  • Base Layers: 2x moisture-wicking thermal tops and bottoms (merino wool or high-performance synthetic).
  • Mid Layers: 1x breathable grid fleece jacket or lightweight down sweater.
  • Outer Shell: Waterproof, windproof jacket and rain pants (Gore-Tex or equivalent).
  • Heavy Insulation: High-loft down jacket (rated to -10°C) for evenings at Base Camp.
  • Footwear: Broken-in trekking boots with ankle support + 4 pairs of merino wool hiking socks.
  • Sleep Gear: Sleeping bag rated to -10°C (teahouse blankets alone are insufficient at 4,130m).
  • Trail Essentials: Telescopic trekking poles (invaluable for stone staircases), 300+ lumen headlamp with spare batteries, polarized UV sunglasses, and high-SPF sunscreen.
  • Hydration & Health: 2x 1L reusable water bottles, purification tablets, personal first-aid kit, blister care (moleskin), and altitude medication.
  • Electronics: 10,000–20,000 mAh power bank (cold temperatures drain batteries quickly).

Gear Rental: High-quality down jackets and sub-zero sleeping bags can be rented in Pokhara or Kathmandu for just $1–2 USD per day.

Safety on the Annapurna Base Camp Trek

  • Check mountain weather forecasts every morning before departing the lodge.
  • Use trekking poles to absorb joint impact and maintain balance on wet stone staircases.
  • Drink 3 to 4 liters of purified water daily to aid acclimatization.
  • Strictly avoid alcohol at elevation; alcohol dehydrates and masks early symptoms of altitude sickness.
  • Protect skin and eyes against intense high-altitude UV radiation.
  • Know when to stop or descend: never push upward through worsening AMS symptoms.
  • Carry comprehensive travel insurance that explicitly covers high-altitude trekking up to 4,500m and helicopter evacuation.
  • Confirm emergency communication protocols with your lead guide prior to starting.

Who Is This Trek Best For?

  • Beginners: Fully achievable with 4–6 weeks of cardio and stair training; choose an 8- to 10-day itinerary with sensible daily pacing.
  • Solo Travelers: Legally requires a guide, making this a safe, companionable, and straightforward trek for solo adventurers.
  • Families and Active Seniors: Well-maintained trails and teahouses make ABC accessible; ensure pacing matches the group's comfort.
  • Experienced Hikers: A fast, rewarding Himalayan journey with close-up 360-degree mountain panoramas without the 3-week commitment of the Circuit.

What You Will See on the ABC Trek

The route offers unparalleled visual variety:

  • Towering Massifs: Annapurna I (8,091m), Annapurna South (7,219m), Hiunchuli (6,441m), and Machhapuchhre / Fishtail (6,993m) from intimate, close-up vantage points.
  • The Annapurna Sanctuary: A glacial bowl amphitheater surrounded by a continuous wall of snow-capped peaks.
  • Traditional Settlements: Gurung and Magar villages with stone architecture, terraced barley fields, and rich Buddhist-Hindu cultural tapestries.
  • Dynamic Ecosystems: Subtropical bamboo valleys, moss-draped oak groves, and vibrant spring rhododendron forests.
  • Rivers and Waterfalls: The dramatic Modi Khola canyon with rushing glacial rivers and hanging waterfalls.
  • Natural Geothermal Springs: The riverside hot springs of Jhinu Danda.

Annapurna Base Camp Trek Pros and Cons

Advantages

  • Dramatic 360-degree mountain amphitheater at Base Camp.
  • Genuinely diverse landscapes compressed into a single 7–12 day route.
  • Rich cultural immersion in Gurung and Magar mountain communities.
  • No weather-dependent mountain flights required (drive from Pokhara).
  • Natural geothermal hot springs at Jhinu Danda on the return leg.
  • Highly established teahouse network with hot meals and beds.

Challenges

  • Altitude above 4,000 m presents real physiological risk.
  • Thousands of steep stone staircases test knee joints and quads.
  • Weather can shift rapidly, bringing afternoon clouds or snow.
  • Lodge accommodation is basic and unheated near Base Camp.
  • Peak autumn season (October–November) brings crowded trails.
  • Descent retraces the same Modi Khola river valley.

How to Prepare for the ABC Trek

Preparation should begin 4 to 8 weeks prior to departure:

  • Physical Conditioning: Build leg strength with stair-climbing, lunges, and weekend practice hikes.
  • Travel Insurance: Secure a policy that explicitly covers trekking up to 4,500 m and helicopter evacuation in Nepal.
  • Logistics: Finalize your registered agency, licensed guide, and ACAP permits in advance.
  • Schedule Buffer: Leave 1–2 flexible days in your overall Nepal travel itinerary for weather delays.
  • Cash Reserves: Withdraw all necessary Nepali Rupee cash in Kathmandu or Pokhara before entering the trail.

Responsible Travel in the Annapurna Sanctuary

The Annapurna Sanctuary is both ecologically sensitive and culturally sacred to local Gurung and Magar communities.

  • Leave No Trace: Pack out all non-biodegradable waste, batteries, and plastics.
  • Eliminate Single-Use Plastics: Use water purification tablets or reusable filters instead of purchasing plastic bottles.
  • Cultural Etiquette: Dress modestly in villages, ask permission before photographing residents, and pass religious mani stones and chortens on the left (clockwise).
  • Ethical Porter Welfare: Ensure your agency respects fair wage standards, provides warm mountain clothing, and enforces the strict 20 kg porter weight ceiling.

ABC Trek Booking Guide

For peak autumn (October–November) and spring (April–May) departures, book your expedition 2 to 4 months in advance to guarantee teahouse reservations and licensed guide availability.

When comparing packages, ensure your operator provides clear transparency on:

  • Government registration and NMA licensed guide credentials.
  • Inclusion of all ACAP permits and official agency TIMS documentation.
  • Fair porter treatment standards and medical protocols.
  • Clear refund and contingency policies in the event of weather disruption or altitude descent.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a TIMS card still needed for the Annapurna Base Camp Trek?
It's more nuanced than a flat yes or no. The old individual-trekker (green) TIMS card was retired when solo trekking ended in April 2023 — that part is genuinely discontinued. But an Agency/Group TIMS (NPR 2,000) is still officially required by TAAN/NTB regulations and is normally processed electronically by your registered agency alongside your guide's paperwork. Trail checkpoints mainly check your ACAP permit and guide credentials in practice, which is why so many sources oversimplify this to "TIMS is dead" — it isn't, it's just been folded into the agency side of booking rather than something you carry as an independent trekker.
Can I trek Annapurna Base Camp solo, without a guide?
No, not legally. A licensed guide booked through a registered agency has been mandatory in the Annapurna region since April 2023. You can still trek without other trekkers around you — "solo" in the sense of not joining a group — but not without a guide.
Do I need any vaccinations before trekking to Annapurna Base Camp?
Nepal doesn't legally require any vaccine for most travelers, aside from yellow fever for those arriving from an endemic country. That said, health authorities commonly recommend being up to date on routine vaccines (MMR, Tdap, polio) plus typhoid and Hepatitis A specifically for travel to Nepal, given food- and water-borne disease risk. This isn't medical advice — check with a travel health clinic 4–6 weeks before departure, since some vaccines need time to become effective.
Is there mobile network or SIM card coverage along the trail?
Coverage exists at lower and mid-route villages (Ghandruk, Chhomrong) through Nepali carriers (Ncell / NTC), but becomes patchy or unavailable above Deurali and at Annapurna Base Camp itself. Don't rely on mobile data as your primary connectivity — teahouse Wi-Fi (where available, for a fee) is generally more consistent than cellular signal in the higher sections.
What is the minimum age for the Annapurna Base Camp Trek, and can children do it?
There's no official minimum age, but this is a demanding multi-day trek with real altitude exposure, so suitability depends more on a child's walking stamina and altitude tolerance than age alone. Families considering it with children should plan a longer, slower-paced itinerary and discuss specifics with their agency rather than assume a standard package pace will work.
Can vegetarians and vegans eat well on the trek?
Yes, vegetarian food is the norm rather than the exception on this route — dal bhat itself is vegetarian, and most teahouse menus have several plant-based options throughout. Vegan trekkers can generally manage well too, though dishes with dairy (butter tea, cheese) are common enough that it's worth flagging dietary needs to your guide early so they can help navigate menus at each stop.
How much cash should I carry, and can I use a card or ATM on the trail?
Carry enough Nepali cash for your entire trek before leaving Pokhara — ATMs become unavailable past Pokhara, and card payment is essentially non-existent on the trail itself. Budget for daily costs plus a reasonable buffer for extras like Wi-Fi, showers, and charging, which are all cash-only at altitude.
Is the Annapurna Base Camp Trek harder than the Everest Base Camp Trek?
Generally no — ABC reaches a lower maximum altitude (4,130 m vs. 5,364 m), involves less cumulative altitude gain, and is typically rated less physically demanding overall, though ABC's stone-staircase sections are, in places, steeper than anything on the standard EBC route. Neither is technical, and both are achievable by a reasonably fit beginner with proper preparation.
What happens if I develop altitude sickness or get injured and can't finish the trek?
Your guide will assess symptoms and, if needed, arrange descent to a lower altitude — the standard first response for AMS. For more serious situations, evacuation (typically helicopter from higher sections) is arranged through your agency and insurance provider, which is exactly why insurance that explicitly covers high-altitude evacuation is non-negotiable. Cancellation or partial-refund terms for a trek cut short vary by agency — confirm this before booking.
Can I combine the Annapurna Base Camp Trek with Poon Hill or another nearby trek?
Yes — adding the Ghorepani–Poon Hill loop onto the standard ABC route is common and only adds a day or two, since it sits along a natural extension of the trailhead approach. Combining ABC with a separate trek like Mardi Himal in the same trip is possible but requires more planning, since they don't share a direct trail connection the way Poon Hill does.

The Final Conclusion: Is the Annapurna Base Camp Trek Worth It?

For trekkers who want genuine Himalayan scale — an 8,000 m peak up close, a glacial sanctuary, real mountain villages — without EBC's flight risk or the Circuit's longer commitment, yes, absolutely.

The main elements to take seriously rather than romanticize: altitude above 4,000 m, several consecutive long walking days, and basic accommodation near the top. Plan around a verified itinerary, budget realistically, and confirm current permit requirements with a licensed agency.
